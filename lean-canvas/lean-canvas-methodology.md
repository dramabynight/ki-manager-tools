## What is the Lean Canvas?

A one-page business model snapshot adapted by Ash Maurya from Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas.
**Not a plan — a hypothesis capture tool.** Every field is a testable assumption.

Core constraint: fill it in under 20 minutes. If you can't describe your idea on one page, it's too complex to explain.

---

## The 9 Fields

### 1. Customer Segments
**Purpose:** Who pays (customer) vs. who uses (user)? Focus on early adopters — the specific subset most eager for your solution today.

**What to write:** One concrete persona, not a market segment. Early adopters, not the mainstream.

**Good:** "Blog authors who want to monetize their audience" — not "content creators"
**Bad:** "Everyone", "the mainstream market", conflating users with customers

**Key distinction:** Customers pay. Users don't. In a blogging platform, authors are customers; readers are users. In search, advertisers are customers; searchers are users.

**Common mistakes:**
- Targeting the middle of the market instead of early adopters
- Describing a demographic instead of a person with a specific problem

---

### 2. Problem
**Purpose:** The 1–3 most pressing problems your early adopters face. Also: how they currently solve it (existing alternatives).

**What to write:** Real problems you've observed or can validate — not assumed needs. Include current workarounds.

**Good:** "Job seekers can't stand out in competitive markets — they cobble together a CV in Word and hope for the best"
**Bad:** Listing 10 problems, inventing needs, missing the existing alternatives section

**Key principle:** "Problems, not solutions, create spaces for innovation."
Problems connect directly to UVP — your UVP must address problem #1.

**Common mistakes:**
- Listing too many problems (max 3)
- Skipping the "existing alternatives" — this is where the real insight lives
- Assuming problems without talking to customers first

---

### 3. Unique Value Proposition (UVP)
**Purpose:** One clear sentence: why your product is different and worth paying attention to. Under 120 characters.

**Structure — go from feature to outcome:**
- Feature: what you built ("professionally designed templates")
- Benefit: what it does ("eye-catching résumé")
- Outcome: what the customer gets ("landing your dream job") ← aim here

**Good:** "Communicate your idea clearly to key stakeholders — a 1-page model that takes 20 minutes" (Lean Canvas itself)
**Bad:** "The best solution", generic benefit language, feature lists

**Common mistakes:**
- Stopping at the feature or benefit level
- Trying to appeal to everyone
- Using filler phrases ("world-class", "innovative", "seamless")

**Bridges:** Problem → UVP → Solution. Your UVP must be directly traceable to your #1 problem.

---

### 4. Solution
**Purpose:** The simplest possible fix for each problem listed. Not a product roadmap — the MVP concept.

**What to write:** One line per problem. Minimum viable version only.

**Key principle:** "Bind a solution to your problem as late as possible." The solution box is intentionally small on the canvas — entrepreneurs naturally over-invest here before validating problems.

**Good:** Sketch of 3 core features matching 3 problems
**Bad:** Detailed product specs, roadmaps, technology choices

**Common mistakes:**
- Spending too long here before validating problems
- Letting solution passion drive the whole canvas

---

### 5. Channels
**Purpose:** How you reach early adopters — and which scalable channels you'll test.

**What to write:** Early-stage: any channel that gets you in front of real customers. Growth: identify scalable channels even if not yet deployed.

**Good:** "Customer interviews" (early), "content marketing + partnerships" (scale)
**Bad:** Assuming viral growth, "word of mouth" without a mechanism, relying only on paid ads

**Key principle:** "Failing to build a clear path to customers is among the top reasons startups fail."

**Common mistakes:**
- Having no channel strategy at all
- Only listing unscalable channels
- Not distinguishing early-access channels from growth channels

---

### 6. Revenue Streams
**Purpose:** How customers pay you, and at what price. Not an afterthought — pricing is part of the product.

**What to write:** Your pricing model + price point. Include unit economics if possible.

**Key principles:**
- "Price defines your customers" — price signals who this is for
- "Getting paid is the first form of validation"
- "Revenue is the difference between a hobby and a business"

**Good:** Monthly SaaS at €99/month, freemium + premium, marketplace commission
**Bad:** "Free", "TBD", deferring pricing to later

**Common mistakes:**
- Avoiding the pricing decision entirely
- Underpricing relative to value delivered
- Not testing willingness to pay early

---

### 7. Cost Structure
**Purpose:** Real costs over the next 3–6 months: MVP build + ongoing burn rate.

**What to write:** What does it cost to define, build, and launch? What's the monthly burn?

**Good:** "€8K for MVP, €3K/month burn (1 founder, cloud hosting €200)"
**Bad:** 3-year projections, ignoring actual costs, phantom revenue offsetting costs

**Common mistakes:**
- Over-detailed forecasting instead of near-term reality
- Ignoring hidden costs (time, opportunity cost)
- Not calculating how long runway lasts

---

### 8. Key Metrics
**Purpose:** 3–5 numbers that tell you whether the model is working — before you get the sales report.

**Outcome metrics (prefer):** Customer adoption, usage patterns, churn
**Output metrics (avoid):** Lines of code, features shipped, meetings held

**Leading indicators (prefer):** Trial signups, qualified leads, churn rate
**Trailing indicators (use for validation):** Revenue, profit

**By model type:**
- SaaS: LTV, CAC, MRR, churn rate
- Ad-based: DAU/MAU, CTR, CPM
- Marketplace: buyer/seller ratio, average transaction, take rate

**Common mistakes:**
- Tracking too many metrics (pick max 5)
- Measuring outputs instead of outcomes
- Ignoring leading indicators until it's too late

---

### 9. Unfair Advantage
**Purpose:** The one thing that cannot be easily copied or bought. Long-term defensibility.

**Real unfair advantages:**
- Insider information or deep domain expertise
- Expert endorsements or personal authority
- Dream team with rare combination of skills
- Network effects (value grows with users)
- Existing community or customer base
- Organic SEO / content moat
- Proprietary data
- Values-based culture (Zappos example)

**NOT unfair advantages:**
- Passion
- Being first to market ("first-mover advantage is a myth" — Google, Facebook, Apple were all followers)
- Source code or features (anything worth copying will be copied)
- "We're just better"

**The stress test:** Would you still have a business if a competitor copied your code, dropped price to zero, or if Google entered your market?

**If you don't have one yet:** Write your "unfair advantage story" — what you'll build systematically over time (network effects, data, community, brand). This becomes a strategic focus, not a future hope.

**Common mistakes:**
- Leaving it blank (worse than writing your story)
- Confusing competitive advantages with unfair advantages
- Missing non-obvious advantages (community, authority, data)

## How the Fields Connect

```
[Customer Segments] ←→ [Problem] ←→ [UVP] ←→ [Solution]
        ↓                                          ↓
   [Channels] ←──────────────────────→ [Revenue Streams]
        ↓                                          ↓
   (how you reach)                        (how they pay)
        └─────────────→ [Key Metrics] ←────────────┘
                               ↓
                    (validates the model)
                               ↓
                      [Unfair Advantage]
                    (long-term defensibility)

[Cost Structure] underlies the entire model
```

**Critical chain:** Customer Segments → Problem → UVP. If these three don't form a tight, logical sequence, the rest of the canvas won't hold.

## Fill Order

**There is no single right order.** Maurya's recommendation:
1. Start with the box you know best (often Solution — but be aware of Innovator's Bias)
2. The first 3 boxes you fill reveal your cognitive biases
3. Prioritize testing the riskiest assumptions first

**Three risk types every model must address:**
- Customer/Desirability risk (usually highest — "will anyone want this?")
- Market/Viability risk ("can this be a business?")
- Technical/Feasibility risk ("can we build it?")

**Suggested teaching sequence** (Customer → Problem → UVP → Solution → Channels → Revenue → Costs → Metrics → Unfair Advantage) works well for workshops because it front-loads customer empathy before solution thinking.

**Innovator's Bias:** The tendency to start with the solution and work backward to justify it. The canvas is designed to counteract this by making the customer and problem fields larger and more prominent.

---

## Key Principles for Coaching

1. **Problems before solutions** — never fill Solution before Problem and Customer Segments
2. **Specificity beats generality** — "urban freelancers aged 28–38 who invoice clients manually" beats "small businesses"
3. **Existing alternatives matter** — the Problem field is incomplete without them
4. **Price is not optional** — a canvas without Revenue is a hobby, not a business
5. **Unfair advantage is strategic** — if you don't have one, write what you'll build
6. **The canvas is a snapshot, not a plan** — it will change; the goal is to surface assumptions quickly
